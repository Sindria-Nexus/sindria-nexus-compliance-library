# Tailoring Guide: From Template to Evidence

A template becomes readiness in four steps. Skip any of them and you have a
document, not a control.

## 0. First, know which audit you are building toward

**Type 1** tests design at a point in time: does the control exist, is it
approved, does it describe your real systems? A tailored, approved policy
set can pass Type 1.

**Type 2** tests operation over a period, typically 6–12 months: did the
control actually run, every time, for the whole period? That is why every
policy in this library names its evidence and its cadence. A quarterly
review with three quarters of signed records is Type 2 evidence; a policy
adopted last month is not.

The practical consequence: **start generating evidence now, even before
you engage an auditor.** Run the access review this quarter. Fill the
vendor tracker today. Date everything. The observation period cannot be
bought retroactively — it can only be started.

## 1. Make it true

Read every line and delete what you do not actually do. Then add what you do
that the template does not mention. An auditor's first test is simple: does
this document describe the company in front of me? A policy that names tools
you do not use, or omits the system that holds your customer data, fails that
test on page one.

Replace every `[bracketed placeholder]`, including:

- Company name, policy owner, and approver
- The actual systems, vendors, and tools you run
- Real review cadences you can keep (a quarterly review you miss is worse
  than a semi-annual review you hold)

## 2. Assign one owner per policy

Every policy needs a named owner who is accountable for it being followed, not
just written. If the owner leaves, the policy gets a new owner the same week.
"Owned by the team" means owned by nobody.

## 3. Approve it, publish it, train on it

- Approval: the owner and one person above them sign and date it.
- Publication: it lives where the team works, not in a folder nobody opens.
- Training: new hires read the policies that apply to their role in week one;
  the whole team re-reads the security and acceptable-use policies annually.
  Keep the attendance record — that record is evidence.

## 4. Generate evidence as you operate

For each policy, decide *before* you need it what evidence proves it:

| Policy | Evidence that proves it |
|---|---|
| Access control | Joiner/mover/leaver tickets, MFA enrollment report, quarterly access-review sign-off |
| Change management | Pull requests with review, deployment log, rollback record |
| Incident response | Incident tickets, post-incident reviews, tabletop exercise notes |
| Vendor management | Completed vendor-review tracker rows, vendor security reviews, contracts/DPAs |
| Risk assessment | Risk register with owners, dates, and treatment decisions |

Store evidence where you can find it in an hour. "We did that, but we can't
show it" is the same as not doing it, from an auditor's chair.

## Tailor by stage

**Solo founder.** You are every role in the roles table, which makes
segregation of duties your hardest problem. Do not pretend otherwise in
the documents — auditors respect an honest one-person control set and
punish a fictional three-person one. Your compensating control is the
documented self-review checklist: completed *before* deployment or
approval, never after, and filed like any other record. When you hire,
the checklist hands off to a real second person.

**Team of 5–20.** Your risk flips: the controls exist, but nobody owns
them. Assign one named owner per policy now, put the review cadences on a
shared calendar with the policy owner as invitee, and make the quarterly
access review a recurring meeting, not a recurring intention.

## Common tailoring mistakes

- **Copying tool names you do not use.** An auditor's first test is
  whether the document describes the company in front of them. One wrong
  tool name poisons the whole policy's credibility.
- **Cadences you cannot keep.** A quarterly review you miss is worse
  than a semi-annual review you hold. Set the slowest cadence you will
  actually honor, then tighten it when the habit exists.
- **Adopting all eight policies in one afternoon.** Eight approved
  policies with no evidence behind any of them is a worse audit position
  than two policies with three months of records. Adopt in the order the
  controls matrix suggests, and let evidence accumulate behind each one
  before adding the next.

## What "done" looks like

You are ready to talk to an auditor when, for every control you claim, you can
point to three things: the policy that requires it, the record that it
happened, and the person who owns it. If any leg is missing, that is your next
piece of work — and the order to fix it in is: owner, policy, evidence.
